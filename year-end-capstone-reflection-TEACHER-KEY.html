🔑 Teacher Answer Key

Suggested answers, scoring notes, and how to apply the common rubric.

Scoring approach. Each question shows ONE possible strong answer. Student answers may use different wording but should hit the same key ideas. Use the score guide under each question to decide level: 0 = blank/incorrect, 1 = partial, 2 = full.

Part A · Your Best MODEL from the Year

Q1 — Best Model

Open-ended description of student's chosen model.

Look for:
A specific model name (e.g., food web, water cycle, energy conversion diagram, flowering plant reproduction cycle, motion diagram). Bonus: student names the parts clearly (arrows, labels, what each represents). Examples of strong choices: food web with at least 4 organisms + arrows showing energy direction; the 5-step plant reproduction cycle; a stimulus-sense-brain-response diagram.
ScoringProficient: model is named + main parts described accurately. Advanced: explains how arrows and labels work together.
Q2 — Why this model
Look for:
A real reason — not "I just liked it." Examples: "It helped me FINALLY understand where food energy comes from." "It showed me that air can be matter even though I can't see it." Watch for any sign of conceptual breakthrough.
ScoringProficient: links the model to a concept the student now understands.
Q3 — Improvement
Look for:
An identified gap from the original work + new understanding from later lessons. Strong example: "I would add MORE arrows because at the time I only drew one chain. I didn't understand it was actually a WEB."
ScoringProficient: names a specific improvement + connects to growth.
Q4 — Question + steps
Look for:
A clear scientific question (not just "I did the magnet test") + step-by-step procedure. Examples of strong investigations: magnet test, pinwheel speed test, ramp + toy car test, ice melting + dissolving sugar, ruler + sound pitch.
ScoringProficient: question is clear + at least 3 steps in order.
Q5 — Evidence/data
Look for:
Specific observations or data, NOT vague statements. Strong: "I tested 8 items. 2 attracted to the magnet (paperclip + nail). 6 didn't. I noticed all the magnetic items had iron or steel."
ScoringProficient: cites specific data/observations.
Q6 — Surprise & predictions
Look for:
Honest reflection on whether prediction matched reality + what was learned. Strong responses often include a "wrong" prediction that led to insight — exactly what real scientists experience.
ScoringProficient: identifies whether prediction was confirmed/changed + names a takeaway.
CER · Claim
Look for:
A direct, complete-sentence answer to the question the student picked. Stronger CERs reference learning that has actually accumulated over the year (e.g., "Energy from the Sun powers nearly EVERYTHING alive on Earth" pulls from many lessons).
CER · Evidence
Look for:
At least 2 specific examples or data points pulled from real lessons. Bonus: evidence from MULTIPLE units (e.g., a food web example + a magnet test example).
CER · Reasoning
Look for:
A connecting explanation — not just a restatement of the evidence. Strong reasoning includes WHY the evidence supports the claim and may name a pattern.
CER ScoringThis is the highest-stakes question of the year. Use the full rubric for CER/Argument criterion.
Q9 — Growth + 5th-grade goal
Look for:
Genuine reflection — not just "I learned a lot." Strong responses identify a SPECIFIC change in thinking ("I used to think...") AND a real goal (e.g., "I want to do more hands-on investigations" or "I want to be better at writing CERs.").
ScoringProficient: contrasts before/after thinking + names a real goal.

Common misconceptions to watch for:
  • Treating reflection as just "I had fun" — push for specific learning moments.
  • Picking the most recent model/investigation instead of the BEST one — encourage looking across the whole year.
  • Writing vague CERs at year-end — this is the moment to push for specific, cited evidence from multiple lessons.
